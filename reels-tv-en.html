I Built an Instagram Reels App for Google TV — Because Scrolling Reels on a Phone Wasn't Lazy Enough

How a ~30 KB Android TV app turns your remote into a Reels controller — no private APIs, no scraping, just a WebView and a lot of key-event plumbing.

The itch

I watch a lot of Instagram Reels. What I don't love is watching them hunched over a phone. My living room has a perfectly good TV — a Chromecast with Google TV — so the obvious question was: why can't I just lean back and scroll Reels with the remote?

Instagram does have an official TV app now — com.instagram.airwave, launched February 2026 — but it's US-only. In Taiwan it doesn't even show up in the Play Store, so there's no easy way to install it. And Google TV doesn't ship a usable web browser either. Stuck outside the US, I built the thing myself, and it turned out to be a fun little exercise in making a web experience feel like a native lean-back app.

The whole thing is a single Activity and one big injected JavaScript function. It weighs about 30 KB. Here's how it works and, more interestingly, the bugs that made it actually usable.

The core decision: wrap the web, don't reverse the API

There are two ways to build this:

  1. Native UI + Instagram's private API. You'd reverse-engineer the mobile app's internal endpoints, replicate its request signing, and render your own feed. This is a maintenance nightmare, it violates Instagram's terms, and it gets accounts flagged the moment the signatures drift.
  2. A native shell around the real Instagram web app. Load instagram.com/reels in a full-screen WebView, let Instagram's own page fetch and render everything, and just translate the TV remote into the right on-page actions.

I went with option 2. From Instagram's servers, the app looks like a completely ordinary Chrome session — because it is one. No private API, no scraping. The native layer's only job is to make that web page behave like a TV app: full-screen, launcher icon, and a D-pad that does the right thing.

The interesting part: making dialogs work with a remote

The naive version — load the page, forward D-pad presses to the WebView's built-in spatial navigation — falls apart the moment you hit anything interactive. Instagram's comment sheet, share sheet, and two-factor menus are modal overlays, and the WebView's spatial navigation happily lets focus escape the modal to the page behind it. Instagram detects the focus leaving and instantly closes the modal. Every dialog "flashed and vanished."

The fix has two pieces:

  • A MutationObserver on the page watches for a sizeable [role=dialog] appearing or disappearing and reports it to the native layer through a tiny JavaScript bridge.
  • When a dialog is open, the app takes over the D-pad. Instead of letting the WebView move focus, it moves a blue highlight ring between the dialog's own buttons and clicks the highlighted one on OK.

Suddenly you can pick a friend from the share sheet or scroll comments with the remote:

Four bugs that taught me something

1. The "menu opens and instantly closes" bug. On the login screen, pressing OK on Instagram's "Try another method" button made the menu flash and disappear. The culprit wasn't focus this time — it was the WebView synthesizing a double click for a single DPAD_CENTER on Instagram's <div>-based buttons: the first click opened the menu, the second dismissed it. The fix: on login pages, intercept OK and perform exactly one deliberate .click() from the native side, swallowing the synthetic pair.

2. Videos default to muted, and there's no touchscreen to fix it. Instagram autoplays muted. On a phone you tap the speaker; on a TV you can't. Setting video.muted = false didn't stick — Instagram's own state management kept re-muting. The reliable fix was to click Instagram's own unmute button on a short interval until it's unmuted, then stop. That flips Instagram's real state, so it stays unmuted for the session.

3. Input lag. After adding the observer, the remote started feeling sluggish. Instagram's Reels DOM mutates constantly, and my observer was re-scanning the whole document on every single mutation. Debouncing it to a 200 ms window, reporting only on state changes, and capping the expensive one-time scans brought the responsiveness back.

4. Reels wouldn't load on the actual TV — just a blank play button. Everything worked in the emulator, but on a real Chromecast with Google TV the reels stalled and showed no video at all. The logs pointed straight at the hardware: mali_gralloc: Failed to allocate from codec_mm … Out of memory. These low-end Amlogic boxes have a tiny dedicated video-decoder memory pool, and Instagram's desktop feed keeps several <video> elements decoding at once — more than enough to exhaust it. The fix: on a short interval, pause every video except the one centered in the viewport, so only one hardware decoder is ever active. The stalls disappeared and reels play smoothly even while scrolling.

Keeping it reels-only

  • Stay on Reels. Tapping a username in the comments would normally navigate you to a profile — a page with terrible remote support. A small guard redirects any navigation off the allowed paths straight back to the feed.
  • Hide the clutter. The left navigation sidebar and Instagram's on-screen prev/next chevrons are hidden (the chevrons only visually — they're kept clickable so the remote's Up/Down can still trigger them).

Takeaways

The lesson I keep relearning: you don't always need to rebuild the thing — sometimes you just need to re-skin how you talk to it. A WebView plus a few hundred lines of key-event plumbing gave me a lean-back Reels experience that survives Instagram's redesigns, because it's driving Instagram's own UI rather than a brittle private API.

It's not going on the Play Store, and it's not trying to be a product. It's a remote, a TV, and a Saturday afternoon. But now I can veg out to Reels from the couch — and honestly, that was the whole point.
